T1
Company Residency and Central Management & Control (CMAC)
ITAA 1936 s 6(1) · DTA Dual Residency · MLI MAP Tie-breaker
▾
Risk Level
Medium — Manageable
General Rule
A company incorporated in Australia is automatically an Australian resident under ITAA 1936 s 6(1), limb (i) — regardless of where management sits. A non-incorporated company must carry on business in Australia AND have CMAC in Australia (limb ii) or Australian-controlled voting power (limb iii).
Apply Facts To Law
Ask: (1) Where are high-level strategic decisions actually made? (2) Is the board predominantly offshore? (3) Does the foreign jurisdiction (e.g. Canada) also treat the entity as a resident under its domestic law? If yes to (3), the MLI-modified DTA MAP applies — no automatic resolution.
Key Risk Indicators
Fully non-Australian board → CMAC offshore → dual residency risk → MAP uncertainty. Treaty benefits may be denied if MAP fails. Australian directors with genuine authority is the critical mitigation step.
The Law

Under ITAA 1936 s 6(1)[1], a company is an Australian resident if it: (i) is incorporated in Australia; (ii) carries on business here with CMAC in Australia; or (iii) carries on business here with voting power controlled by Australian residents. Limb (i) is automatically satisfied for any company incorporated in Australia — no further conditions apply.

CMAC is determined by where the 'directing mind' makes real decisions: Bywater Investments Ltd v FCT (2016)[2]; Malayan Shipping Co Ltd v FCT (1946)[3]. It is not the formal location of board meetings, but where substantive decisions are actually made. The ATO's position is in TR 97/16[4] and updated in TR 2018/5[5].

Where dual residency arises, the applicable DTA's tie-breaker applies. However, MLI Article 4[6] modified most Australian DTAs so that the prior automatic 'place of effective management' test no longer resolves corporate dual residency; instead a MAP between competent authorities is required. This creates uncertainty — no outcome is guaranteed.

Applying Facts to Law

Step 1 — confirm incorporation jurisdiction. If incorporated in Australia → resident by limb (i). Step 2 — identify any foreign residency claim by the ultimate parent jurisdiction. Step 3 — if dual residency risk exists, apply the relevant DTA (as modified by the MLI). Step 4 — if MAP is required, assess whether UA/the entity can demonstrate Australian CMAC to avoid MAP uncertainty altogether.

To establish Australian CMAC: (a) at least one director in Australia with genuine authority over Australian affairs; (b) key strategic decisions made or formally ratified at board meetings held in Australia; (c) governance documents (board charter, minutes) recording Australian decision-making; (d) alignment with PCG 2018/9[7].

Opinion & Recommendation

Incorporation in Australia satisfies residency under limb (i) regardless of board location. However, a fully non-Australian board creates dual residency risk that must be managed. Appoint at least one Australian-based director with genuine management authority. Ensure key decisions are made or formally ratified in Australia, documented by board minutes and supported by a board charter aligned to TR 2018/5 and PCG 2018/9.

Permanent Establishment — Remote Employees and Dependent Agents
OECD Model Art 5 · BEPS Action 7 · PCG 2023/1 · TR 2002/5
▾
Risk Level
High — Action Required
General Rule
A PE requires: (1) a place of business; (2) that is geographically and temporally fixed; (3) through which the enterprise's business is carried on. Exceptional/temporary home office (e.g. COVID) ≠ PE. Regular, recurring, or indefinite home office arrangements can satisfy all 3 limbs (2025 OECD Commentary).
Apply Facts To Law
Classify each employee by function: (a) Revenue-generating (marketing, sales) — highest PE risk as dependent agents; (b) Core operational (remote for 8+ months or indefinite) — fixed place PE risk; (c) Support functions (IT helpdesk) — lower risk but treaty must be checked. Check specific DTA with each country.
Key Risk Indicators
Employees working indefinitely offshore; marketing staff soliciting or concluding contracts; absence of local entity; no written limits on employee authority. Each jurisdiction's DTA must be separately assessed; BEPS Action 7 narrowed the independent agent exception.
The Law — Fixed Place PE

OECD Model Article 5(1)[8] requires a 'fixed place of business through which the business of the enterprise is wholly or partly carried on'. Three elements are essential: (i) a place of business (physical location); (ii) that is 'fixed' (geographic permanence + temporal permanence, typically 6+ months); (iii) through which the enterprise's core business is conducted (not merely preparatory/auxiliary).

The 2025 OECD Commentary on Article 5[9] clarifies home office PE: exceptional/temporary arrangements (including COVID-related remote work) do not create PEs. However, regular and recurring home office use — where the employer effectively requires or relies on the home as a place of business — can satisfy all three elements. The ATO's approach is in TR 2002/5[10] and updated in PCG 2023/1[11].

The Law — Dependent Agent PE

OECD Model Article 5(5)[8], as amended by BEPS Action 7[12], creates a PE where a person habitually exercises authority to conclude contracts on behalf of the enterprise, or habitually plays the 'principal role' leading to contract conclusion. This is a lower threshold than the pre-BEPS standard. Marketing employees who habitually solicit customers and facilitate sales — even without formally executing contracts — create dependent agent PE risk.

Applying Facts to Law

For each jurisdiction with employees: (1) Identify the applicable DTA and whether it incorporates BEPS Action 7 changes (post-2018 treaties and MLI-modified DTAs will have narrower independent agent exceptions); (2) Assess each employee's actual functions — do they conclude or materially contribute to contracts?; (3) Assess duration of remote working — indefinite arrangements are hardest to defend; (4) Assess whether the employee's home is 'at the disposal' of the employer; (5) Evaluate whether local preparatory/auxiliary exceptions may apply.

  • Marketing/sales employees: likely dependent agent PE in jurisdictions with post-BEPS treaty coverage
  • Employees working 8+ months or indefinitely: likely fixed place PE risk
  • IT helpdesk (internal support): lower risk, but check each specific treaty
Opinion & Recommendation

Material PE risk exists in each jurisdiction with indefinitely remote employees and in any jurisdiction where marketing employees solicit customers or play the principal role in concluding sales. Conduct a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction PE risk assessment with local counsel; limit employee authority in employment contracts; consider establishing local service entities in high-risk jurisdictions; require sales approvals to be made from Australia.

Permanent Establishment — Server / Data Centre as Fixed Place of Business
OECD Model Art 5 · 2025 Commentary paras 122–131 · Core vs Preparatory Functions
▾
Risk Level
Critical — Likely PE Exists
General Rule
A server constitutes a PE if: (1) physically located in the country; (2) it has geographic + temporal permanence; and (3) the enterprise carries on its core business through the server. Servers used only for preparatory/auxiliary functions (data storage, advertising, testing) do NOT constitute a PE under OECD Model Art 5(4).
Apply Facts To Law
Critical question: does the server perform 'core' business functions (sales execution, payment collection, customer contracting) or merely 'preparatory/auxiliary' activities? Servers that autonomously execute sales and collect payments = core business = PE. Duration (2+ years) satisfies temporal permanence. Fixed data centre location satisfies geographic permanence.
Key Risk Indicators
Automated sales processing; autonomous payment collection; customer contracts executed through the server; physical data centre (not cloud); 2+ years of operation. No human presence is required for a server PE — the test is functional, not personnel-based.
The Law

The 2025 OECD Commentary on Article 5, paras 122–131[13], provides the definitive guidance on server PEs. The test is functional: where a server autonomously performs the core business activities of the enterprise (e.g., executing sales contracts with customers, collecting payments), it constitutes a fixed place of business PE. The 'preparatory or auxiliary' carve-out in OECD Model Art 5(4)[8] applies to servers that provide back-office support (data storage, advertising, market information) but not to servers through which the enterprise itself contracts with customers.

There is no requirement that the server be 'attended' — unlike a construction site PE, a server PE can arise with only maintenance staff present, because the revenue-generating activity occurs through automated server processes.

Applying Facts to Law

Apply a two-stage analysis. Stage 1 — fixed place test: (a) Is the server physically situated at a fixed location in the country? (b) Has it been there for a sufficient period (typically 6+ months; 2 years is clearly sufficient)? Stage 2 — core business test: (a) Does the server process sales to local customers?; (b) Does it execute or complete payment transactions?; (c) Are these core income-generating functions rather than data storage or advertising? If all elements satisfied → strong probability of PE.

Where a PE is established, the profits attributable to that PE must be quantified under the authorised OECD approach (AOA) — typically by reference to the functions performed, assets used, and risks assumed by the PE.

Opinion & Recommendation

Where servers perform automated sales and payment collection — the core revenue-generating functions of the business — a PE is very likely. Urgently obtain local tax law advice to assess PE registration and tax return obligations. Quantify potential past tax liabilities. Evaluate commercial feasibility of moving to a cloud-based model. Disclose the PE risk to any acquirer as a material due diligence finding with appropriate representations and warranties in the transaction documentation.

Withholding Tax Shortfall — Royalties Paid to Non-Treaty Recipient
ITAA 1936 s 128B · ITAA 1997 s 26-25 · Voluntary Disclosure PS LA 2012/5
▾
Risk Level
Critical — Underpaid WHT + Deduction Denial
General Rule
Australian WHT applies to royalties paid by Australian residents to non-residents at 30% (default) under ITAA 1936 s 128B. A reduced DTA rate applies only if the recipient is actually a resident of the DTA partner country. Applying a reduced treaty rate to a non-treaty recipient is a WHT shortfall, triggering underpaid WHT, penalties, interest, and potential deduction denial under ITAA 1997 s 26-25.
Apply Facts To Law
Step 1: Determine whether the payment is a 'royalty' under ITAA 1936 s 6(1) (broad definition — includes IP licences, software, know-how). Step 2: Identify the actual recipient's country of residence. Step 3: Check whether Australia has a DTA with that country. Step 4: Apply the correct WHT rate (DTA rate if treaty country; 30% otherwise). Step 5: Calculate shortfall = (correct rate − applied rate) × gross royalty.
Key Risk Indicators
Payment routed through intermediary to access a lower treaty rate (treaty shopping); recipient country is not the country whose DTA rate was applied; royalty incorrectly characterised (e.g. as a service fee to avoid WHT); deduction taken without correct WHT withheld. Post-PepsiCo [2025] HCA 30 — ATO broadly interprets royalties.
The Law

Section 128B, ITAA 1936[14] imposes WHT on royalties paid by Australian residents to non-residents. Section 128D[15] makes this WHT a final tax. The default rate is 30% under Income Tax Rates Act 1986, s 12. A reduced DTA rate applies only where the recipient is a resident of the treaty partner country — the reduced rate cannot be applied to residents of non-treaty countries, even if the payment is channelled through a treaty country.

Under ITAA 1997 s 26-25[16], a deduction for royalties (or other non-resident payments) is denied to the extent the payer failed to withhold and remit the correct WHT. This is a significant commercial consequence beyond the WHT shortfall itself.

Voluntary disclosure under PS LA 2012/5[17] before ATO audit/review significantly reduces the shortfall penalty under TAA 1953 s 284-75[18] — penalties range from 25% (lack of reasonable care) to 90% (intentional disregard), reduced substantially for proactive disclosure. FCT v PepsiCo Inc [2025] HCA 30[19] confirmed the breadth of the royalty WHT obligation.

Applying Facts to Law

Fact-application process: (1) Characterise the payment — is it a 'royalty' under s 6(1) ITAA 1936 (see also TR 2024/D1[20] for software/IP guidance)? (2) Identify the actual recipient — who received the funds (follow the money, not the contract name)? (3) Identify the recipient's tax residence — does Australia have a DTA with that country? (4) Apply the correct WHT rate. (5) Calculate shortfall + interest (under TAA 1953 s 280-15) + penalty. (6) Consider whether treaty shopping anti-avoidance provisions (principal purpose test under MLI Article 7) may apply where the structure appears to have accessed a treaty rate through an intermediary.

Where WHT shortfall is identified, make a voluntary disclosure under PS LA 2012/5 before lodging amended returns. Quantify the shortfall, deduction disallowance, GIC, and penalties. Disclose the liability in any transaction documentation (purchase price adjustment or tax indemnity).

Opinion & Recommendation

Where the actual recipient of a royalty is resident in a non-treaty country, the full 30% WHT rate applies and any shortfall creates immediate WHT debt, deduction denial, GIC, and penalty exposure. A voluntary disclosure under PS LA 2012/5 is strongly recommended before any ATO audit or transaction. Quantify the full liability (WHT shortfall + deduction denial + GIC + penalty) and address via transaction indemnity or completion adjustment.

CGT on Disposal of Foreign Shares — Australian Resident Company
ITAA 1997 ss 104-10, 115-10 · Division 855 · Subdivision 768-G · CGT Discount Unavailable
▾
Risk Level
Critical — Full Gain Assessable; No Discount
General Rule
Australian resident companies are taxed on CGT gains from all CGT assets worldwide (ITAA 1997 ss 100-25, 104-10). Division 855 exempts non-TAP gains — but only for FOREIGN residents. Australian resident companies do NOT benefit from Division 855. Companies are also ineligible for the 50% CGT discount (ITAA 1997 s 115-10). Full gain is assessable.
Apply Facts To Law
Step 1: Confirm taxpayer is Australian resident (if yes → worldwide taxation; Division 855 inapplicable). Step 2: Is there a Subdivision 768-G active business exemption? (requires ≥10% ownership interest in foreign company). Step 3: No CGT discount for companies. Step 4: Full capital gain = proceeds − cost base. Step 5: Identify if return is incorrect → voluntary amendment obligation.
Key Risk Indicators
Misapplication of Division 855 to an Australian resident taxpayer; reliance on AI (ChatGPT) or unverified analysis for a tax position; ownership below 10% (no 768-G exemption); companies incorrectly applying 50% CGT discount; failure to amend prior-year returns. No reasonably arguable position → enhanced penalties.
The Law

Under ITAA 1997 ss 100-25 and 104-10[21], Australian resident companies are subject to CGT on all CGT events occurring on worldwide CGT assets. CGT event A1 (disposal of an asset — s 104-10) applies on the sale of shares. The capital gain equals proceeds minus the cost base.

Division 855[22] disregards gains or losses from 'non-taxable Australian property' (non-TAP) — but this exemption is available only to foreign residents. An Australian resident company cannot access Division 855 regardless of the nature of the asset disposed of.

Subdivision 768-G[23] provides an active foreign business exemption for Australian companies holding ≥10% participation interests in foreign companies. Where the ownership interest falls below 10%, the exemption is unavailable and the full gain is assessable. The 50% CGT discount (ITAA 1997 s 115-10[24]) does not apply to companies (available only to individuals, trusts, and complying superannuation funds).

Where a prior-year return is incorrect, a voluntary amendment under TAA 1953 Part IVC[25] and voluntary disclosure under PS LA 2012/5 reduces penalty exposure under TAA 1953 s 284-75. A position taken without a reasonably arguable position — including one based on AI-generated analysis — does not qualify for the 'reasonably arguable position' reduced penalty base under TAA 1953 s 284-15.

Applying Facts to Law

Structured analysis: (1) Confirm whether the disposing entity is an Australian or foreign resident (critical — determines whether Division 855 is even available to consider); (2) Apply Division 855 only if the entity is a foreign resident — if Australian resident, proceed to step 3; (3) Check Subdivision 768-G — is the ownership interest ≥10%? If not, exemption unavailable; (4) Calculate the full capital gain (proceeds − cost base); (5) Confirm that the CGT discount does not apply (companies are excluded under s 115-10); (6) Assess whether an amended return and voluntary disclosure is required; (7) Quantify total liability including tax, GIC, and penalties and address via transaction indemnity if relevant.

Opinion & Recommendation

Australian resident companies cannot access Division 855. Where the ownership interest in the foreign company is below 10%, Subdivision 768-G also does not apply. The full capital gain is assessable and companies are ineligible for the CGT discount. A prior-year return based on Division 855 applied to an Australian resident contains a fundamental legal error. Voluntarily amend the return and make an early disclosure. Quantify the tax liability and address in any transaction documentation as a specific indemnity. Never rely on AI-generated analysis (ChatGPT or similar) as the basis for a tax position without independent expert verification.

Transfer Pricing — Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) for Double Taxation
Division 815 ITAA 1997 · DTA Article 25 · MLI Article 17 · Bilateral APA
▾
Risk Level
High — Urgent MAP Filing Required
General Rule
Where the ATO makes a transfer pricing primary adjustment and the counterpart foreign jurisdiction refuses a corresponding adjustment, economic double taxation results. The MAP under the applicable DTA's Article 25 provides the mechanism for bilateral resolution. MLI Article 17 obligates treaty partners to provide corresponding adjustments in these circumstances, subject to MAP.
Apply Facts To Law
Step 1: Confirm primary adjustment has been accepted. Step 2: Confirm the counterpart jurisdiction's refusal of corresponding adjustment. Step 3: File MAP request with the ATO within the DTA time limit (typically 3 years from first notification). Step 4: ATO engages with foreign competent authority (e.g. CBDT for India). Step 5: Consider bilateral APA for future inter-company transactions.
Key Risk Indicators
MAP time limits (usually 3 years) are strict — late filing forfeits treaty relief. MAP can take 2–4 years but is the primary resolution mechanism. Bilateral APA provides future certainty. Double taxation creates cash flow burden. Post-acquisition, new owners must continue MAP or lose right of treaty relief.
The Law

Australian transfer pricing rules are in ITAA 1997 Division 815[26], which requires cross-border dealings to be priced on arm's length terms. Where the ATO makes a primary upward adjustment to Australian income, the corresponding entity in the other jurisdiction should receive a downward adjustment — if they do not, the same income is taxed twice (economic double taxation).

Article 25 of the applicable DTA[27] provides a Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) to resolve such disputes. MLI Article 17[28] requires corresponding adjustments to be made by treaty partners where a primary adjustment has been made, and obliges competent authorities to undertake MAP to eliminate double taxation. The OECD MAP Manual (November 2025)[29] sets out procedural requirements including filing timelines.

Recent case law confirms that Australian courts may stay domestic recovery proceedings while MAP is ongoing, providing procedural relief: Oracle Corporation [2025] FCAFC 145.[30]

Applying Facts to Law

Action steps: (1) File MAP request with the ATO promptly (confirm time limit under applicable DTA — typically 3 years from first notification of the primary adjustment); (2) The ATO will engage as Australian competent authority with the foreign competent authority to seek bilateral agreement; (3) Consider seeking a stay of ATO domestic recovery action while MAP is pending (see Oracle Corporation); (4) For future cross-border transactions with the related party, initiate a bilateral Advance Pricing Arrangement (APA) with both tax authorities to provide prospective certainty; (5) Disclose the outstanding double taxation exposure in any M&A transaction documentation and factor in the time/cost/uncertainty of MAP as part of valuation or indemnity allocation.

Opinion & Recommendation

Foreign Dividends — Non-Assessable Non-Exempt (NANE) Income, FITO, and Debt Integrity Rules

▾
Risk Level
High — Exposure Under New DDCR & Thin Cap Rules
General Rule
Dividends from foreign subsidiaries to Australian corporate groups are treated as non-assessable non-exempt (NANE) income under s 768-5 if participation tests (≥10%) are met. Otherwise, they are assessable, and double taxation is mitigated via a Foreign Income Tax Offset (FITO) under Division 770. The regimes are mutually exclusive. Outgoings to fund these investments can be deducted via s 25-90 but are subject to strict limits under thin capitalisation and debt deduction creation rules.
Apply Facts To Law
Verify participation percentage (≥10% for NANE; <10% for assessable + FITO). For s 25-90 interest claims, apply the new Division 820 earnings-based Fixed Ratio Test (capped at 30% of Tax EBITDA). Simultaneously audit funding structures against the Subdivision 820-EAA Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR) to guarantee that borrowings do not arise from associate-related asset transfers or payments.
Key Risk Indicators
Relying unconditionally on s 25-90 deductions without applying the 30% Tax EBITDA cap; falling foul of DDCR Type 1 (borrowing to acquire an asset from an associate) or Type 2 (borrowing to fund an associate payment/distribution) arrangements; and miscalculating the Division 770 cap on portfolio investments.
The Law — NANE Treatment & Interest Deductions

Section 768-5 of the ITAA 1997 establishes the participation exemption, which explicitly deems certain foreign dividends to be NANE income. To successfully qualify for NANE treatment, five concurrent criteria must be met:

  • The recipient must be an Australian-resident company.
  • The recipient must maintain a direct or indirect 'participation interest' of at least 10% in the paying entity.
  • The distribution must be paid by a 'foreign company' (non-Australian resident).
  • The foreign entity must not be classified as a Controlled Foreign Company (CFC) at the exact time of the distribution.
  • The dividend must not be sourced or derived from profits attributable to the foreign company's Australian branch activities.

Crucially, achieving NANE classification under s 768-5 unlocks the specific deduction pathway in ITAA 1997 s 25-90. While the initial legislative proposal to repeal s 25-90 was dropped, outgoings (such as interest on borrowings) used to buy foreign subsidiaries are now heavily regulated by Division 820. Deductions are subject to the Fixed Ratio Test (FRT), limiting net debt deductions to 30% of Tax EBITDA, and face complete disallowance if the funding architecture triggers the **Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR)** under **Subdivision 820-EAA**.

The Law — Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR Integrity)

Under Subdivision 820-EAA, debt deductions that satisfy s 25-90 will still be entirely denied if they fall into either of the two targeted debt creation schemes:

  • Type 1 (Asset Acquisition): An entity incurs a debt deduction to an associate to fund or facilitate the acquisition of a CGT asset (or legal/equitable obligation) from an associate.
  • Type 2 (Payments/Distributions): An entity borrows from or through an associate to fund or facilitate a payment or distribution (such as a dividend, capital return, or royalty) to an associate pair.

The ATO applies strict compliance oversight to any corporate restructuring or financing shift designed to bypass these rules, as outlined in PCG 2025/2.

The Law — Foreign Income Tax Offsets (FITO)

Where an Australian corporate entity fails to meet the 10% participation threshold (e.g., portfolio investment holdings), NANE treatment is blocked. In these circumstances, the foreign dividend becomes fully assessable income in Australia, and the group must seek tax relief via ITAA 1997 Division 770.

Section 770-10 grants an entitlement to a FITO equal to the amount of foreign income tax paid (including foreign withholding tax and eligible underlying corporate taxes) on the assessable amount. However, this offset is strictly capped by s 770-75, which establishes the Australian tax liability that would otherwise be payable on that specific foreign segment. Furthermore, s 770-75 explicitly confirms that no FITO can be claimed for foreign taxes paid on sums classified as NANE income.

Applying Facts to Law

To accurately assess corporate foreign distributions and linked debt deductions, apply this updated structured approach:

Step 1 — Verify Holdings: Audit the exact associate-inclusive direct and indirect corporate interest held in each paying foreign subsidiary to confirm if it sits above or below the 10% barrier.

Step 2 — NANE Processing: For subsidiaries at or above 10%, document compliance with s 768-5 to confirm NANE status. Do not assign foreign tax credits or prepare a FITO schedule for these streams.

Step 3 — Division 820 & DDCR Filtering: Map all borrowing costs historically claimed under s 25-90 against the Subdivision 820-EAA rules. Confirm that the debt was not used to acquire assets from associates or fund distributions to associates. Run the Fixed Ratio Test to ensure total net debt deductions fit within the 30% Tax EBITDA threshold.

Step 4 — Portfolio Processing: For holdings below 10%, declare the gross distribution as assessable income. Collate verified receipts of foreign tax actually paid and execute a Division 770 calculation to apply the necessary FITO limit cap.

Opinion & Recommendation

CONCLUSION: While s 25-90 interest deductions remain valid alongside NANE treatment under s 768-5, they are no longer an "unlimited safe harbor." The introduction of the Thin Capitalisation Fixed Ratio Test and the strict mandates of the Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR) mean that historical international financing arrangements must be completely re-vetted to prevent sudden disallowance of interest deductions.

Recommendation: Perform an immediate audit of all legacy international funding channels using the risk matrices provided in PCG 2025/2. Establish a dynamic Tax EBITDA calculation model to closely monitor compliance with the 30% cap, and maintain a pristine equity-tracking registry to isolate NANE streams from portfolio streams subject to Division 770.

Cross-Border Loan — Debt/Equity, Deductibility, Withholding Tax, Transfer Pricing & Thin Capitalisation

▾
Risk Level
High — Strict Earnings/Documentation Thresholds
General Rule
Inbound related-party funding must be characterised as a debt interest under Div 974 to permit interest deductions. Deductions are subject to arm's length transfer pricing pricing requirements (Subdiv 815-B), strict Interest Withholding Tax (IWT) remittance, and structural limitation under the Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR) and Thin Capitalisation rules (Div 820).
Apply Facts To Law
Verify the existence of an effectively non-contingent obligation (ENCO) to repay principal and interest. If debt status is satisfied, benchmark the interest rate to establish an arm's length condition, verify that the funds were not used in an associate asset acquisition (DDCR), and calculate the 30% Tax EBITDA threshold under the Fixed Ratio Test (FRT).
Key Risk Indicators
Loans lacking fixed repayment terms or containing solvency-contingent clauses; related-party interest rates exceeding arm's length benchmarks; unremitted IWT causing deduction denial; debt deductions exceeding $2m paired with a high-leverage or low Tax EBITDA profile; or funding used for internal group asset transfers.
1.1 Characterisation & Deductibility — Div 974, s 8-1 & s 25-90

An inbound advance must first pass the multi-limbed test under s 974-20(1) to be characterized as a debt interest. This requires a scheme that constitutes a financing arrangement, the receipt of a financial benefit, and a crucial effectively non-contingent obligation (ENCO) under s 974-135 to provide a future financial benefit that is at least equal to the benefit received. If repayment is contingent on borrower solvency or lender discretion, it may fail the ENCO test, recharacterising the instrument as equity and rendering any returns non-deductible distributions.

Where debt status is achieved, interest is generally deductible under the general deduction provisions of s 8-1. While paragraph 8-1(2)(c) ordinarily denies deductions for expenses incurred in producing non-assessable non-exempt (NANE) income, s 25-90 provides a specific statutory override. It preserves deductions for interest expenses incurred to fund the acquisition of foreign subsidiary shares that generate NANE dividend income under s 768-5.

1.2 Compliance & Transfer Pricing — s 128B & Subdiv 815-B

Every interest payment to a non-resident triggers an Interest Withholding Tax (IWT) liability under s 128B at a statutory rate of 10%, unless reduced by an applicable Double Tax Agreement (DTA). Under s 26-25, the borrower is hit with an absolute denial of its interest deduction if it fails to withhold and remit these funds within the statutory timeframes set by s 128C. The deduction remains blocked until the underlying shortfall is completely rectified and paid to the Commissioner, alongside running General Interest Charge (GIC).

Concurrently, Subdivision 815-B requires the pricing of the loan to reflect wholly independent, arm's length conditions. If the interest rate exceeds what an independent party would pay in comparable circumstances, the ATO will identify a "transfer pricing benefit" under s 815-120 and substitute an arm's length rate under s 815-115(1), permanently disallowing the excess deduction. To maintain a Reasonably Arguable Position (RAP) and avoid severe administrative penalties under s 284-75(2), contemporaneous benchmarking documentation matching the guidelines in TR 2014/6 must be finalized prior to lodging the tax return.

1.3 Thin Capitalisation & DDCR — Division 820

For general class investors with total debt deductions exceeding the $2 million de minimis threshold in s 820-35, Division 820 imposes a strict sequence of structural limits. First, the Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR) under Subdivision 820-EAA (s 820-423A) must be evaluated. The DDCR completely disallows debt deductions if the loan proceeds were utilized to acquire a CGT asset or a legal/equitable obligation from an associate entity. This anti-avoidance check operates as a hard gate before any thin capitalisation capacity tests are run.

If the DDCR does not apply, the remaining debt deductions face the modern thin capitalisation tests:

  • Fixed Ratio Test (FRT): The default statutory mechanism under s 820-50(1)(a). It caps net debt deductions at 30% of "Tax EBITDA" (as defined in s 820-51). Any excess disallowed interest can be carried forward for up to 15 years under s 820-56.
  • Group Ratio Test (GRT): An alternative elective option under s 820-46(3), allowing highly leveraged entities to substitute the 30% cap with a limit based on the worldwide consolidated group's third-party interest-to-EBITDA ratio.
  • Third Party Debt Test (TPDT): An elective test under s 820-46(4) that restricts debt deductions strictly to third-party funding arrangements. Choosing the TPDT automatically results in the complete disallowance of all related-party debt deductions, making it highly unsuitable for cross-border related-party loans.
Applying Facts to Law

Execute this sequence of diagnostic reviews when assessing related-party debt arrangements:

  • Step 1 — Verify Debt Interest & ENCO: Confirm the presence of an absolute, unconditional obligation to repay principal. Eliminate any terms that link repayment to borrower performance or solvency.
  • Step 2 — Track Funds Use: Audit the cash flow path. Ensure funds were not used to purchase assets from an associate (triggering the DDCR) and trace any NANE-producing asset acquisitions to verify that s 25-90 applies.
  • Step 3 — Run Transfer Pricing Benchmarking: Perform independent credit rating and interest rate benchmarking to establish an arm's length rate before lodgement, protecting the company's RAP.
  • Step 4 — Calculate Tax EBITDA Capacity: Model the company's Tax EBITDA under s 820-51. Determine if the net debt deductions sit within the 30% FRT cap, and evaluate if a 15-year carry-forward asset needs to be recognized.

1.1 CFC Status and the Active Income Test (AIT) — Part X

Under ITAA 1936 s 340, a foreign company is classified as a Controlled Foreign Company (CFC) if five or fewer Australian 1% entities hold an aggregate associate-inclusive control interest of 50% or more[cite: 14]. If an Australian entity holds a direct or indirect participation interest of 10% or more, it becomes an attributable taxpayer subject to the cross-border compliance overlay of Part X[cite: 14]. Where the CFC resides in a prescribed listed country (such as France), it can completely escape accruals attribution under the Active Income Test (AIT) pursuant to s 432[cite: 14]. Passing this test keeps untainted profits outside Australia's immediate tax base until a dividend is actively declared[cite: 14].

1.2 Dividend Pathways and NANE Exemptions

  • Pathway A — Previously Attributed Profits (s 23AI): If the CFC failed its AIT in a prior period and the parent was already taxed on those undistributed earnings under accruals rules, a subsequent dividend allocation paid out of those exact profits is treated as Non-Assessable Non-Exempt (NANE) income under s 23AI to the extent of the accompanying attribution debit[cite: 14].

This requires that the recipient is a valid Australian corporate tax entity and holds a direct or indirect participation interest of at least 10% at the exact time the distribution is made[cite: 14]. Meeting this threshold historically or post-distribution will not suffice[cite: 14].
1.3 Withholding Taxes (FITO Disallowance) and Conduit Foreign Income (CFI)

A material tax risk arises when a foreign jurisdiction (such as France) imposes local withholding tax (WHT) at source on incoming distributions[cite: 14]. Because the underlying dividend is categorized as NANE income in Australia under either pathway, **Division 770** explicitly blocks the parent from claiming a Foreign Income Tax Offset (FITO)[cite: 14]. The foreign WHT becomes a permanent, irrecoverable bottom-line cash cost[cite: 14]. Tax calculations must verify relevant bilateral treaties to ensure WHT is reduced to the lowest available treaty rate (often capped at 5% for qualifying corporate shareholders holding 10% or more)[cite: 14].

To prevent further tax friction when repatriated profits are passed on to global stakeholders, the parent should look to utilize **Conduit Foreign Income (CFI)** rules under **Division 802**[cite: 14]. By tracking NANE inflows inside a dedicated CFI account, the Australian entity can formally elect to distribute these foreign-sourced profits onward to its non-resident shareholders completely free from Australian dividend withholding tax, mitigating cross-border tax leakage[cite: 14].

CONCLUSION: Dividends received by an eligible Australian corporate parent from a listed-country CFC are typically non-assessable non-exempt (NANE) under s 768-5 or s 23AI, provided participation and tracing criteria are satisfied[cite: 14]. Recommendation: Perform strict date-of-distribution audits to ensure the 10% participation test is flawlessly maintained when the dividend is paid[cite: 14]. Finally, ensure the company updates its corporate tracking schedules and utilizes Division 802 Conduit Foreign Income mechanisms to safeguard onward distributions to non-resident shareholders from triggering unnecessary Australian dividend withholding tax obligations[cite: 14].

1.1 Withholding Tax (WHT) Compliance & Deduction Bans

Under **ITAA 1936 Division 11A (ss 128A–128NB)**, non-residents are subject to Australian withholding tax on dividends, interest, and royalties (unless specific statutory exemptions under s 128B(3) apply)[cite: 15]. This obligation is managed through the PAYG system under **Subdivision 12-F of Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953**[cite: 15]. Domestic non-withheld default rates stand at 30% for dividends and royalties, and 10% for interest; however, bilateral Double Tax Agreements (DTAs), subject to Multilateral Instrument (MLI) variations, routinely reduce these caps as follows[cite: 15]:

  • Dividends: Typically capped at 5% for qualified corporate parents (holding ≥10% or ≥5% depending on the specific treaty) or 15% for portfolio holdings[cite: 14, 15].
  • Interest: Standardized at 10% under most treaties, with certain agreements allowing 0% for unrelated financial institutions[cite: 13, 15].
  • Royalties: Generally compressed to a 5% to 10% range, or entirely exempt in isolated modern treaty contexts[cite: 15].

1.2 Thin Capitalisation Rules — Post-2023 Earnings Stripping Framework

**Division 820 of the ITAA 1997** curtails base erosion by capping debt deductions (interest and economically equivalent outgoings) for inbound and outbound entities where funding profiles breach statutory limits[cite: 15].   • Fixed Ratio Test (FRT): The default mechanism, which strictly caps net debt deductions at **30% of Tax EBITDA**[cite: 15]. Any deductions denied under this ratio are permanently disallowed and cannot be carried forward[cite: 15].
  • Group Ratio Test (GRT): Enables highly leveraged entities to claim debt deductions up to their localized share of the worldwide group’s net third-party interest expense relative to global macro EBITDA[cite: 15].
  • Third-Party Debt Test (TPDT): Operates to allow a full deduction of interest expenses, provided they are incurred on genuine, arm's length third-party debt that meets strict statutory criteria (e.g., zero associate dealings and security restricted to Australian assets)[cite: 15].
1.3 Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR) & Instrument Classification

Operating completely independent of the standard thin capitalisation thresholds, the **Debt Deduction Creation Rules (DDCR)** under **Division 820B** apply to arrangements from 1 July 2024[cite: 15]. The DDCR targets transactions without economic substance by permanently disallowing interest deductions on debt used to fund payments to related non-resident associates, or to acquire assets from related non-residents, where the arrangement increases debt deductions without generating a corresponding taxable amount in Australia[cite: 15]. This includes scenarios where an Australian entity borrows from an offshore related party to fund dividends, capital returns, or intra-group asset transfers back to the overseas corporate group[cite: 15].

This overlay requires careful coordination with **Division 974** debt/equity classification rules[cite: 15]. To secure an interest deduction and fall within withholding frameworks rather than franking and dividend rules, the funding arrangement must represent a genuine debt interest by satisfying the **effectively non-contingent obligation (ENCO)** test—requiring the issuer to provide a financial benefit equal to or greater than face value at maturity[cite: 15]. Hybrid instruments must be reviewed carefully to avoid reclassification[cite: 15].

1.4 Conduit Foreign Income (CFI) Protections

To eliminate double withholding tax friction for multinational groups using Australia as a regional hub, **Division 802** provides the Conduit Foreign Income (CFI) framework[cite: 15]. Where an Australian corporate entity receives foreign-sourced income that is unassessed locally (such as NANE dividends under Subdivision 768-A), it can make a formal CFI designation at the time of declaring or paying an onward dividend to its own non-resident shareholders[cite: 14, 15]. These CFI-designated distributions are fully exempt from Australian dividend withholding tax[cite: 14, 15]. These elections must be made concurrently with the distribution and cannot be applied retrospectively[cite: 15].

Applying Facts to Law

  • Step 4 — Execute Division 974 Debt Testing: Review hybrid and intra-group loan agreements to confirm they satisfy the ENCO threshold for characterization as a genuine debt interest[cite: 15].
  • Step 5 — Synchronize CFI Accounts: Track all NANE inflows and execute formal CFI elections at the exact time of dividend declaration to protect non-resident shareholders from dividend withholding tax[cite: 14, 15].
Opinion & Recommendation

CONCLUSION: Modern Australian international tax rules heavily restrict the use of highly leveraged corporate structures[cite: 15]. Failing to satisfy withholding tax remittances triggers absolute deduction disallowances under s 26-25, while breaching the post-2023 thin capitalisation or post-2024 DDCR frameworks leads to the permanent loss of debt deductions on related-party arrangements[cite: 15].

Recommendation: Implement an integrated cross-border payment compliance framework to monitor withholding tax obligations and protect operating deductions[cite: 15]. Prepare contemporaneous thin capitalisation workpapers to model the earnings-stripping tests, and structurally review all related-party transactions to ensure compliance with the DDCR rules[cite: 15]. Finally, embed strict conduit foreign income (CFI) account monitoring workflows into your treasury systems to eliminate dividend withholding tax leakage on the redistribution of foreign earnings to offshore parent entities[cite: 14, 15].

The Law

Under ITAA 1997 Division 855, a foreign resident may generally disregard capital gains and losses from a CGT event unless the underlying asset is Taxable Australian Property (TAP). Section 855-10 establishes that a foreign resident disregards a capital gain or loss where the CGT event does not happen in relation to TAP. TAP is defined to include: (a) taxable Australian real property (TARP); (b) an indirect Australian real property interest; and (c) assets used in carrying on a business through an Australian permanent establishment (PE). Shares in an Australian company that do not fall within these categories are not TAP, and any capital gain is therefore disregarded.

A membership interest (e.g., shares) constitutes an indirect Australian real property interest under s 855-25 only where two cumulative tests are both satisfied:

  • Non-Portfolio Interest Test (s 960-195): The foreign resident (plus associates) holds 10% or more of the entity's interests at disposal or over a 12-month look-back period within the 24 months preceding disposal. This test is satisfied if the sum of direct and associate-inclusive control interests reaches 10% or more, regardless of whether voting rights attach.
  • Principal Asset Test (s 855-30): More than 50% of the market value of the entity's assets is attributable to Australian real property (TARP). Where the entity holds no Australian real property, this test fails and the shares cannot be TAP. The principal asset test is the pivotal filter: where the company's business is predominantly intellectual property, software, data analytics, or other non-real-property assets, the test will fail.

The Foreign Resident Capital Gains Withholding (FRCGW) regime under TAA 1953 Sch 1 Subdiv 14-D requires the purchaser to withhold 15% of the purchase price on acquisition of certain assets from a foreign resident, including indirect Australian real property interests. Where the shares are not TAP (i.e., the principal asset test is not satisfied), no FRCGW obligation arises.

Applying Facts to Law

In applying the law to the facts of the transaction, follow these structured steps:

Step 1 — Non-Portfolio Test: Confirm whether the selling entity's aggregate associate-inclusive interest at or within 24 months of disposal reached 10%.

Step 2 — Principal Asset Test: Conduct a principal asset test by reference to the total market value of the target company's assets at the time of disposal. Where the entity is a software or technology company with no real property holdings, the principal asset test will ordinarily fail.

Step 3 — Look-Through Review: Confirm there are no 'look-through' assets (e.g., subsidiaries holding Australian real property) that could cause the test to be satisfied indirectly at the upper level.

Step 4 — Conclusion on TAP & Withholding: If the principal asset test fails, the shares are not TAP, Division 855 allows the foreign resident to disregard any gain or loss, and no FRCGW withholding is required from the purchaser.

Step 5 — Record Keeping: Retain contemporaneous documentation of the asset composition and valuation, including valuation support for non-real property assets, in case of an ATO review.

Opinion & Recommendation

CONCLUSION: Where the target Australian company holds no real property and the principal asset test is not satisfied, a foreign resident shareholder will disregard any CGT gain on disposal under s 855-10 and no FRCGW withholding obligation arises for the purchaser.

Recommendation: Ensure that robust, contemporaneous documentation of the company's asset composition and independent market valuations for the software/intellectual property assets are secured and archived prior to completion to easily discharge the burden of proof in the event of an ATO review.

Under TASA 2009 ss 30-35 and 30-40[48] (effective 1 July 2024), registered practitioners must self-report significant Code breaches to the TPB. Advising concealment of a known tax shortfall is arguably a significant breach. Failure to self-report is a further independent breach and may attract separate sanctions.

Following a TPB investigation under Subdivision 60-E, TASA 2009[49], the following sanctions may be imposed. The 'fit and proper person' test under s 20-5, TASA 2009[50] will also be reviewed.

Sanction Provision Likelihood Assessment
Written caution s 30-15[51] Supplementary only Insufficient for deliberate concealment advice
Orders (ethics course etc.) s 30-20[52] Possible supplementary May accompany suspension
Suspension of registration s 30-25[53] Likely Advising concealment is a fundamental integrity breach; suspension is the expected primary sanction
Termination of registration s 30-30[54] Possible Where TPB finds deliberate advice to obstruct tax administration; cannot be excluded
Criminal sanctions TAA 1953 Possible If conduct amounts to aiding/abetting tax evasion or failure to comply with self-reporting obligation
Opinion & Recommendation

Advising a client to conceal a known tax shortfall from the ATO constitutes multiple serious breaches of the TASA 2009 Code of Professional Conduct: honesty and integrity (s 30-10(1)), compliance with taxation laws (s 30-10(2)), duty to advise clients of obligations (s 30-10(5)), not obstructing tax administration (s 30-10(13)), and the 2024 Determination Code item 17. Suspension of registration is the most likely primary sanction; termination is possible in serious cases. The self-reporting obligation under ss 30-35 and 30-40 should be assessed by the practitioner. Incoming management should replace the tax agent immediately and engage new advisers with appropriate compliance governance.

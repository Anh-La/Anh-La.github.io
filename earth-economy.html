The Liquidity Lens on Global Output

P = L × (P/L)

Real GDP ($112.4T) measures what the world produces. Asset prices (P) trade on something else entirely: the stock of Global Liquidity (L) — central bank, shadow-bank and cross-border credit — multiplied by risk positioning (P/L). Since roughly 80% of global lending is collateral-backed, financial stress tends to originate from a collateral/liquidity shortage rather than from a growth shortfall.

Where the Cycle Stands

CrossBorder Capital's Global Liquidity Index (GLI, 0–100 scale) tracks a repeating ~65-month sine wave. The last full trough was December 2022; by early 2026 several trackers reported the dollar-denominated pool topping out near $188.8–193.5T with growth now rolling over — implying a multi-quarter liquidity headwind into 2027 as a 2026–2028 debt-refinancing wall absorbs cash. This chart is an illustrative rendering of the framework, not a live feed.

What's Adding Liquidity

  • Fed formally ended quantitative tightening (QT) in 2026
  • PBoC sustained easing — large monthly injections earlier in the cycle
  • Treasury bill-heavy issuance ("Treasury QE") frees bank balance-sheet capacity

What's Draining Liquidity

  • Treasury General Account (TGA) rebuild after the debt-ceiling resolution: ~$919B and rising
  • US dollar strength and firmer bond volatility impair collateral value
  • 2026–2028 debt-maturity wall forces refinancing that competes for scarce liquidity

The Core Tension for 2026

Central banks face a genuine trade-off: inflation control argues for continued restraint, while financial-stability risk — a maturing debt wall, thinning repo-market cushions, and collateral scarcity signaled by rising bond volatility — argues for renewed accommodation. Howell's framework treats this as a recurring, roughly 65-month rhythm rather than a one-off event: liquidity floods in to refinance debt, inflates asset prices, then drains as central banks respond to the resulting inflation, and the cycle repeats.

Key Structural Observations

🏭 Services dominance: Services account for 65.2% of global output, driven by financial services, technology and professional sectors in advanced economies.
⚙️ Industrial rebalancing: Industrial share has declined from 35% (2010) to 30.5% as manufacturing shifts to emerging economies and automation displaces labour.
🌱 Agricultural efficiency: Agriculture represents only 4.3% of global output despite feeding 8.25 billion people — a measure of sustained productivity growth since the Green Revolution.
💧 Liquidity sensitivity: Services (financial services, technology) carry the highest beta to swings in Global Liquidity, since valuations lean on discount rates and credit availability rather than physical output. Industry sits in the middle via capex and trade-credit cycles. Agriculture is the least liquidity-sensitive sector — output is driven more by weather and physical supply than by the credit cycle.

Key Findings

  • No petrodollar "backing" system was ever created
  • Eurodollars emerged in the 1950s–60s, predating 1971
  • Oil shocks tested — but did not create — the system
  • Collateral shortages drive real liquidity crises
  • Official policy transmission is limited and indirect
  • Asset prices strongly feedback into Eurodollar capacity
Focus on bank balance sheets, collateral velocity, and offshore dollar claims — not commodity invoicing.

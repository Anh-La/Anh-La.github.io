Global Liquidity Framework
Michael Howell's liquidity-first view: markets as debt-refinancing machines, where crises come from collateral shortages, not mispricing.
P = L × (P/L)

Asset prices (P) are driven by the stock of Global Liquidity (L) — central bank + shadow-bank + cross-border credit flows — multiplied by risk positioning (P/L). Because ~80% of lending is collateral-backed, a shortage of good collateral, not a lack of earnings, is what triggers financial crises.

Where the Cycle Stands

CrossBorder Capital's Global Liquidity Index (GLI, 0–100 scale) tracks a repeating ~65-month sine wave. The last full trough was December 2022; liquidity was projected to peak near an index level of ~70 around mid-2026, and by early 2026 several trackers (TFTC, Capital Wars) reported the dollar-denominated pool topping out near $188.8–193.5T with growth now rolling over — implying a multi-quarter liquidity headwind into 2027 as a 2026–2028 debt-refinancing wall absorbs cash. This chart is an illustrative rendering of that framework, not a live feed.

What's Adding Liquidity

  • Fed formally ended quantitative tightening (QT) in 2026
  • PBoC sustained easing — RMB 2.7tn (~$380bn) injected in a single month earlier in the cycle
  • Treasury bill-heavy issuance ("Treasury QE") frees up bank balance-sheet capacity

What's Draining Liquidity

  • Treasury General Account (TGA) rebuild after the debt-ceiling resolution: ~$919B and rising
  • US dollar strength and firmer bond volatility (MOVE index) impair collateral value
  • 2026–2028 debt-maturity wall forces refinancing that competes for scarce liquidity

The Core Tension for 2026

Central banks face a genuine trade-off: inflation control argues for continued restraint, while financial-stability risk — a maturing debt wall, thinning repo-market cushions, and collateral scarcity signaled by rising bond volatility — argues for renewed accommodation. Howell's framework treats this as a recurring, roughly 65-month rhythm rather than a one-off event: liquidity floods in to refinance debt, inflates asset prices, then drains as central banks respond to the resulting inflation, and the cycle repeats.
